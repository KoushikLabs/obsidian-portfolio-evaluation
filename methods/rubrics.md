# Rubrics

## What it is

A **rubric** is *"a framework that sets out criteria and standards for different
levels of performance and describes what performance would look like at each
level"* (Dart et al. 1998; Davidson 2004 — the definition the
[[external-sources/norad-portfolio-mel-guide-summary|Norad/Itad guide]]
itself uses). In evaluation, a rubric makes the meaning of "good" (and
"adequate", and "poor") **explicit and agreed in advance**, so that an evaluative
judgement is transparent and defensible rather than implicit and arbitrary.

A rubric has two axes:
- **Criteria** — the dimensions you care about (e.g. relevance, reach,
  contribution to outcomes, learning generated).
- **Standards / performance levels** — a described scale (e.g.
  excellent / good / adequate / poor) with a **word picture** of what each level
  looks like for each criterion.

## Why it matters for portfolio evaluation

Rubrics are arguably the single most important tool for the **heterogeneity
problem** (open question 1) and for **credibility at scale** (open question 3):

- They let you judge **unlike interventions against shared standards** without
  forcing them onto a single numeric indicator. A small advocacy grant and a
  large service-delivery grant can both be rated against "contribution to
  portfolio outcomes," with level descriptions appropriate to each.
- They make portfolio-level judgement **robust and auditable**. As the Norad/Itad
  guide notes, at portfolio level *"the evidence may be contradictory or
  ambiguous, and ... assessing progress will therefore require a judgement.
  Rubrics can be a useful way of structuring these judgements, and ensuring they
  are robust."*
- They are the natural scoring layer on top of **cross-case analysis** and
  **Value for Investment**.

## How to build one (quick recipe)

1. Derive criteria from the **portfolio theory of change** and the evaluation
   questions.
2. Define a performance scale (3–5 levels).
3. Write a concrete word-picture for each cell (criterion × level).
4. Pilot it on 2–3 real cases; calibrate so different assessors agree.
5. Apply across the portfolio; record the evidence behind each rating.

## Limitations / cautions

- A rubric encodes value judgements — **who sets the standards matters**. Co-design
  with funders/grantees to avoid imposing ill-suited criteria.
- Word-pictures must be specific enough to drive consistent ratings; vague levels
  reintroduce arbitrariness.
- Calibration across assessors is essential for credibility.
