# Contribution Analysis

## What it is

**Contribution Analysis** (John Mayne) is a theory-based approach to causal
inference. Instead of trying to *attribute* an outcome to an intervention
(usually impossible at portfolio scale), it builds a **credible, evidence-backed
"contribution story"**: a reasoned argument that the intervention plausibly made
a difference, given the theory of change, the evidence, and the other factors at
play.

## How it works (the six steps)

1. **Set out the attribution problem** to be addressed.
2. **Develop the theory of change** and its assumptions and risks.
3. **Gather existing evidence** on the theory of change.
4. **Assemble and assess the contribution story** — and the challenges to it.
5. **Seek out additional evidence** where the story is weak.
6. **Revise and strengthen** the contribution story.

The output is a contribution claim with an explicit confidence level, naming the
assumptions that hold and the rival explanations that have been ruled out (or
not).

## Why it matters for portfolio evaluation

- It is the **default causal logic** of credible portfolio evaluation: the realistic
  claim is "the portfolio *contributed to* these higher-level outcomes," not
  "the portfolio *caused* them."
- It depends on a **portfolio theory of change** — which is exactly what the
  [[external-sources/norad-portfolio-mel-guide-summary|Norad/Itad guidance]]
  puts at step 1 of portfolio MEL. The portfolio ToC is the spine; contribution
  analysis is how you test it.
- It directly addresses **open question 3** (credibility at scale): it gives you a
  defensible way to make a contribution claim and be transparent about confidence.

## When to use it

- Portfolio-wide evaluations asking "what is the funder's overall contribution?"
- Influence/advocacy and systems-change portfolios where attribution is hopeless.
- *Caveat from Norad's guide:* where most funding flows to multilaterals or as
  core funding, even contribution analysis may not be fully possible — be honest
  about that limit.

## Limitations / cautions

- Only as good as the theory of change behind it.
- Requires deliberately seeking **disconfirming** evidence and rival explanations,
  not just assembling a flattering narrative.
- Resource-intensive to do rigorously.
